Takeaway

McCarthy's keynote from the 2018 Live Sound Summit reads as a philosophy of system tuning more than a how-to. He opens with the Beatles at Shea Stadium as the field's ground-zero failure, then frames audio as a mix of artistic and scientific sides whose objective questions live mostly in shades of gray. The argument turns on four positions worth holding: if measurement contradicts your ear, find the reason rather than dismissing the analyzer; think about the whole space, since every speaker affects every seat; analyze arrays as sums of their parts using A + B = AB; and resist gross over-EQ, because narrow filters in high frequencies produce a one-seat solution that helps no one else. McCarthy closes with Q&A on coverage-mic placement, standing waves as ordinary reflections, and M-noise as a successor specification for maximum SPL.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with FFT analyzers (Smaart, SIM, Open Sound Meter) at a conceptual level
  • Awareness that PA systems cover audiences with multiple speakers, not one
  • Basic understanding of EQ filters (frequency, gain, Q)

By the end you'll be able to

  • Distinguish subjective from objective decisions in system tuning and recognize where shades of gray live
  • Apply the A + B = AB principle: analyze arrays by their individual components before judging the sum
  • Justify why gross narrow-band EQ produces a one-seat solution, citing McCarthy's filter expiration argument
  • Diagnose whether a measurement anomaly is expected system behavior or a genuine fault, using the Mr. Spock heuristic

Questions

  1. 1.McCarthy splits audio into subjective and objective sides and then locates most "objective" questions in shades of gray. Describe what he means by shades of gray, using his examples of stereo aim and polarity reversal, and explain why even an apparently binary choice rarely produces one right answer for the whole room.Understand

    The subjective side (mixing, artistic choices) has very few clear answers and that is fine. The objective side (system tuning, distribution to the audience) seems like it should have clear answers but most of them turn out gray. Stereo aim is gray because aiming left and right at zero degrees benefits roughly a thousand center seats and hurts nine thousand side seats; the "right" aim depends on which fraction of the audience you favor. Polarity reversal is gray because correctness depends on phase, time, and which seat you are evaluating: a flip can be right in one zone and wrong in another. McCarthy's progress test settles it: a solution that helps more seats than it hurts counts as progress. Treating a one-seat fix as a universal fix is the failure mode McCarthy is warning against.

  2. 2.Walk through McCarthy's A + B = AB formula. Why does he insist on measuring individual speakers before evaluating an array, and what does he claim he has never seen in 36 years of FFT analysis? What follows for an engineer staring at a 24-box hang that "sounds weird"?Analyze

    The formula says an array's behavior at any point equals the sum of the individual speakers' contributions at that point, given each speaker's amplitude and phase. McCarthy claims that across 36 years of FFT analysis he has not seen an array do anything not predictable from its individual components. If a 24-box hang sounds weird, the diagnosis path is to measure one box at a time and rebuild the sum mentally; the array is not a black box with emergent magic. An engineer who only looks at the summed response cannot tell whether a dip reflects the system or one specific box misbehaving, and applying gross EQ to the sum without component data is guesswork. McCarthy's claim is a falsifiable engineering position: any engineer who finds a counterexample should report it.

  3. 3.McCarthy calls "measures good but sounds bad" his favorite cop-out and uses the bridge analogy to defend the measurement. Defend or push back: when, if ever, is the engineer's ear a more reliable instrument than a calibrated FFT analyzer, and what does it cost the field to take McCarthy's hard line?Evaluate

    A defense of McCarthy's position notes that the field has many ways to fool itself (expectation effects, fatigue, single-seat bias), and a calibrated measurement is one of the few defenses against believing your ears too much. The bridge analogy lands because the cost of being wrong about structural steel forces honesty in civil engineering, and audio loses nothing by adopting the same posture. A pushback might point out that the analyzer measures one mic position at one moment with one stimulus, while the ear integrates many positions over time. McCarthy himself opens a door for this: when measurement and hearing diverge, find the cause (Thelma vacuuming, a pillar in front of the mic, an I-beam in front of the speaker). The hard line is "do not stop investigating," not "the analyzer is always right." Look for an answer that distinguishes those two positions.

  4. 4.You are tuning a small theater and notice a 6 dB peak at 4 kHz and a 4 dB peak at 80 Hz at your front-of-house measurement position. Based on McCarthy's filter expiration argument and his rare use of narrow EQ, which peak gets the filter, how wide is it, and what is your reasoning for ignoring or leaving the other?Apply

    The 80 Hz peak gets the filter. Low frequencies have long wavelengths, so the structures that produce them (room modes, speaker-speaker summation, speaker-boundary interactions) extend across large physical areas. A wide filter at 80 Hz (an octave or wider) will help much of the audience because the same low-end issue exists across many seats. The 4 kHz peak is McCarthy's "ice sculpture": a narrow correction at the FOH mic position will help that one seat and hurt the seats around it, because at 4 kHz the wavelength is short enough that the interference pattern changes within inches. Reasoning explicitly from filter expiration: the 80 Hz filter has the longest expiration date because the underlying acoustic structure is large; the 4 kHz filter expires the moment you move your head. McCarthy uses five filters max, mostly octave-wide, almost never narrower than half an octave, and treats narrow high-frequency cuts as a net loss: the correction expires the moment you move your head.
