Takeaway

McCarthy's keynote from the 2018 Live Sound Summit reads as a philosophy of system tuning more than a how-to. He opens with the Beatles at Shea Stadium as the field's ground-zero failure, then frames audio as a mix of artistic and scientific sides whose objective questions live mostly in shades of gray. The argument turns on four positions worth holding: if measurement contradicts your ear, find the reason rather than dismissing the analyzer; think about the whole space, since every speaker affects every seat; analyze arrays as sums of their parts using A + B = AB; and resist gross over-EQ, because narrow filters in high frequencies produce a one-seat solution that helps no one else. McCarthy closes with Q&A on coverage-mic placement, standing waves as ordinary reflections, and M-noise as a successor specification for maximum SPL.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with FFT analyzers (Smaart, SIM, Open Sound Meter) at a conceptual level
  • Awareness that PA systems cover audiences with multiple speakers, not one
  • Basic understanding of EQ filters (frequency, gain, Q)

By the end you'll be able to

  • Distinguish subjective from objective decisions in system tuning and recognize where shades of gray live
  • Apply the A + B = AB principle: analyze arrays by their individual components before judging the sum
  • Justify why gross narrow-band EQ produces a one-seat solution, citing McCarthy's filter expiration argument
  • Diagnose whether a measurement anomaly is expected system behavior or a genuine fault, using the Mr. Spock heuristic

Watch for

  • 8:32 the bridge analogy: if a bridge measures unsafe but looks fine, you do not cross it; audio deserves the same respect for measurement
  • 11:18 the A + B = AB formula introduced: a 24-box array is 24 individual relationships, not one mystery object
  • 23:04 the filter expiration date argument: low frequencies hold up across a venue, high frequencies melt into one-seat solutions
