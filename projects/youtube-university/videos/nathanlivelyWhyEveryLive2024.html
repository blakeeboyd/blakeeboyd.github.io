Why Every Live Sound Engineer Should Embrace the Humble Checklist

Takeaway

Lively reads Atul Gawande's Checklist Manifesto and asks what live event production should take from medicine, aviation, and construction. His argument: pro audio has gotten specialized fast (audio, lighting, video, staging, then A1, A2, system tech, RF tech, network tech inside audio alone), and complexity creates room for small things to fall through the cracks. The wired backup microphone that never got set up is a story about a missing system. He walks through a sample pre-show audio checklist on GitHub (outcome-focused, short, every input and output verified end-to-end), demos a collaborative checklist app, and closes on the goal of shifting the industry from heroic individuals toward processes that make good shows inevitable.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Some exposure to live event production roles (A1, system tech, project manager)
  • Awareness that small oversights at load-in can cascade into show-time failures
  • Comfortable with the idea that workflow is part of the engineer's craft

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain why complexity and specialization create the need for checklists in live production
  • Distinguish an outcome-focused checklist from a step-by-step procedure
  • Draft a five-to-nine-item pre-show audio checklist for a specific show context
  • Evaluate the trade-off between relying on experienced individuals and relying on documented systems

Questions

  1. 1.Lively borrows Gawande's argument that complexity drives the need for checklists. Restate that argument in your own words and apply it to live event production. What kinds of specialization has the industry developed in the last few decades, and how does that specialization create new failure modes?Understand

    A strong response restates Gawande's claim: as a field becomes more specialized, the number of moving parts grows faster than any one practitioner can hold in working memory, and small omissions become routine. In pro audio, the response should name the specializations Lively lists (audio, lighting, video, staging, pyro at the department level; A1, A2, A3, system tech, RF tech, network tech within audio) and identify failure modes introduced by specialization: handoffs between specialists, cross-department coordination, single-engineer shows where the one person is also their only check.

  2. 2.Compare the "heroes" model (rely on the experienced engineer to remember everything) with the "systems" model (rely on a documented checklist). What does each model handle well, and where does each break down? Which kinds of shows are most exposed to the failure modes of the heroes model?Analyze

    The heroes model handles novel situations well because an experienced engineer can adapt in ways no checklist anticipates. It breaks down on routine items where attention is the limiting factor, especially on small shows where the engineer is alone and juggling. The systems model handles routine reliably and survives staff turnover, but a checklist cannot decide what to do when something unexpected happens, and it can create false confidence if items get ticked without verification. Shows most exposed to heroes-model failures are small and medium ones where one engineer covers the whole department: no assistant to remind, no second pair of eyes, every item lives in one head.

  3. 3.Draft a five-to-nine-item pre-show audio checklist for a show context you know (a club gig, a corporate event, a theater performance, a sports broadcast). Follow Lively's rules: focus on outcomes rather than procedure, keep each item short, and stay within working-memory limits.Apply

    A strong response produces a checklist of five to nine items, each phrased as an outcome rather than a step (Lively's example: "every input shows up in the console where expected, free of noise and distortion"). Items should be the things that hurt most if they get missed. Good responses will include items like backup paths verified, all outputs heard end-to-end, talkback tested both directions, recording armed and rolling, intercom checked with every position. The list should be readable in under a minute and avoid procedural steps that the engineer already knows by habit.

  4. 4.Lively argues the industry should move responsibility off individuals and onto processes, so that good shows become inevitable rather than dependent on the right person being in the room. Defend or push back on this position. What does the systems-first view miss, and what does the heroes-first view miss?Evaluate

    A strong response engages both sides. For systems: documented processes survive staff turnover, scale across shows, distribute responsibility so no one person is the single point of failure, and free experienced engineers to spend attention on the genuinely novel parts of a show. Against pure systems thinking: checklists do not catch problems they were not written to catch, can create complacency, and can become so long they stop being read. For heroes: experienced judgment recognizes novel failure modes and improvises in ways no document can. Against pure heroes thinking: it does not scale, it punishes the practitioner who is the one allowed bad day, and it slows the industry's collective learning because individual lessons stay individual. A reasonable position holds both: checklists for the routine and the catastrophic, judgment for the rest.
