Takeaway

Professor Messer walks through the full binary procedure for IPv4 subnetting. Convert the IP and subnet mask to binary, perform a bitwise AND to find the network address, set the host bits to all ones to find the broadcast address, and count the numbers in between as the usable host range. He works two examples (192.168.1.165 with /24, then 10.11.12.13 with /16) to show the procedure is identical regardless of where the mask falls. The closing section sets up a problem that becomes recognizable to anyone who has seen his later Seven Second Subnetting video: a remote site is handed 192.168.1.0/24 by the home office and needs four networks of 40 devices each, which works out to a /26 split. The video shows the long-form binary derivation of all four resulting subnets (.0, .64, .128, .192) and their host ranges. This is the chronological predecessor to his shortcut method and the source of the math that the shortcut hides.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with binary math (converting between decimal and 8-bit binary)
  • Awareness that an IPv4 address has four octets, each 0-255
  • Comfort with bitwise operations (AND, OR)

By the end you'll be able to

  • Convert any IPv4 address and subnet mask between decimal and binary
  • Apply a bitwise AND to derive the network address from an IP and mask
  • Compute the broadcast address by setting all host bits to 1
  • Subnet a given /N block into smaller subnets to fit a known network-count and host-count requirement

Watch for

  • 3:28 the bitwise AND procedure with its full rule set. 1 AND 1 brings down 1, anything else brings down 0
  • 9:12 the design question that motivates subnetting. The ISP hands you a block and you have to decide how to split it
  • 14:24 the four /26 subnets enumerated by walking the two extra bits through 00, 01, 10, 11

Questions

  1. 1.The subnet mask is the tool that separates an IPv4 address into a network portion and a host portion. Explain in your own words how a contiguous string of ones followed by zeros performs that separation, and walk through why a /24 mask gives you 254 usable hosts on the subnet (not 256).Understand

    A subnet mask is 32 bits long, with some number of contiguous ones at the left followed by zeros. Where the mask is 1, the corresponding bit of the IPv4 address belongs to the network portion: its value is fixed for every host on this subnet. Where the mask is 0, the corresponding bit belongs to the host portion: its value varies across the hosts on the subnet. A /24 mask has 24 network bits and 8 host bits, so the 8 host bits can take 2^8 = 256 values. Two of those values are reserved: the all-zeros value identifies the subnet itself (the network address) and the all-ones value is the broadcast address. The remaining 254 values are usable host addresses.

  2. 2.Apply Messer's procedure to a new problem: 172.16.45.200 with subnet mask 255.255.255.224 (a /27). Find the network address, the broadcast address, and the first and last usable host addresses. Show the binary work for the fourth octet.Apply

    /27 means 27 bits in the mask: three additional ones in the fourth octet beyond /24, so the fourth-octet mask is 11100000 = 224. Convert 200 to binary: 11001000. Bitwise AND of 11100000 with 11001000 = 11000000 = 192, so the network address is 172.16.45.192. To find the broadcast, set the five host bits to ones: 11011111 = 223, so the broadcast is 172.16.45.223. The first usable host is one above the network address (172.16.45.193), and the last usable host is one below the broadcast (172.16.45.222), giving 30 usable hosts on this /27 subnet (2^5 minus 2).

  3. 3.You are given 10.50.0.0/24 by your ISP and you need to fit 4 networks of 40 devices each. Walk through Messer's table comparison: why does /25 fail, why does /27 fail, and why is /26 the right choice? Identify the constraint each rejected option violates.Apply

    /25 (255.255.255.128) borrows one bit from the host portion and gives 2 subnets of 126 hosts each. It fails the network-count constraint: 2 is less than 4. /26 (255.255.255.192) borrows two bits and gives 4 subnets of 62 hosts each. It satisfies both constraints: 4 networks matches the requirement and 62 hosts per subnet exceeds the 40 needed, with comfortable headroom. /27 (255.255.255.224) borrows three bits and gives 8 subnets of 30 hosts each. It fails the host-count constraint: 30 is less than 40, even though the network count is more than enough. A strong answer notes Messer's pattern: each step down doubles the network count and roughly halves the per-subnet host count, so the right choice is the leftmost option on the table that satisfies both constraints simultaneously.

  4. 4.Compare this video's long-form binary procedure to Messer's later Seven Second Subnetting shortcut method. What does the long form give an engineer that the shortcut cannot, and where is the shortcut the right tool? Identify at least one problem the long form can solve that the shortcut cannot.Analyze

    The long form grounds the calculation in the underlying binary structure. The engineer sees that /26 means "two additional network bits in the fourth octet," that the four subnets correspond to the four combinations 00, 01, 10, 11 of those bits, and that 62 hosts comes from 2^6 minus 2. That understanding generalizes. The seven-second shortcut replaces the binary work with two pre-drawn lookup tables and a small rule set: faster under exam pressure, and a smaller error surface for the four standard answers (network, broadcast, first usable, last usable). Where the long form pays off and the shortcut does not: variable-length subnet masking (subnets of different sizes inside the same block), supernetting (combining smaller blocks into a larger one), checking whether two arbitrary hosts on different masks can reach each other directly, and reasoning about /30 point-to-point links or /31 and /32 edge cases. The long form belongs to understanding and non-standard problems. The shortcut belongs to timed exams.
