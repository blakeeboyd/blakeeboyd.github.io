Delay Always Means Phase Shift But Phase Shift Does Not Always Mean Delay

Dan Worrall · 09:17 · Applied

Audio Theory / Signal Processing, Filter Theory

eqphase

Watch on YouTube ↗

Takeaway

Worrall corrects a common misunderstanding about minimum-phase EQs: the phase shift they introduce is not the same as a time delay. He sets up a 100 Hz sine wave in Reaper and produces cancellation three different ways. First by inverting polarity (no delay, perfect cancellation across all frequencies). Second by nudging the waveform forward 5 ms, which is half a cycle at 100 Hz but would actually shift a 200 Hz signal a full cycle and put it back in phase. Third by running it through an all-pass filter set at 100 Hz, which produces cancellation in the middle of the tone but reveals on the waveform that the filter is anticipating, ringing, and rearranging the signal in time rather than delaying it. He closes with a sharp spike at the end of the test tone, which passes through the all-pass filter with its peak amplitude almost doubled. This is why EQ cuts can sometimes make a track louder rather than quieter.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with the concepts of phase and amplitude
  • Some awareness of DAW operations (delay, EQ plugins)
  • Exposure to comb filtering as a result of summed signals

By the end you'll be able to

  • Distinguish three methods of producing 180° phase shift: polarity, delay, and all-pass filter
  • Explain why delay always means phase shift but phase shift does not always mean delay
  • Describe filter ringing and why minimum-phase filters ring only after the impulse
  • Explain how phase rearrangement can make an EQ-cut signal louder in peak amplitude
