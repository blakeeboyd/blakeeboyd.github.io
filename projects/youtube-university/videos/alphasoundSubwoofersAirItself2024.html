Subwoofers (and the air itself!) are NOT in-phase with your mixer and amplifier.

Takeaway

Sheets mounts an 18-inch B&C driver in a warehouse wall as an infinite baffle and measures it with four sensors at once: voltage across the terminals, current on the speaker wire, a laser displacement reader pointed at the cone, and a condenser test mic an inch from the dustcap. The setup answers a question audio people argue about: when you compare a console output trace to a measurement mic trace, why does the phase relationship look wrong? The answer is in three stacked surprises. AC-coupled analog I/O adds positive phase shift to low frequencies, so the very tools used to measure subs corrupt the reading unless a DC-coupled interface is in the chain. The driver itself lags up to 180 degrees behind the voltage signal once frequency rises above its resonance, because cone mass and surround stiffness resist motion. And the condenser mic reads another 180 degrees out of phase with the driver because microphone diaphragms respond to air pressure, which is the second derivative of cone displacement. Slow-motion footage of the cone with LED voltage indicators confirms the math. Contributions from Filippo Bartolozzi at B&C and engineers at Nexo are credited.

Questions

  1. 1.Sheets opens with a battery-and-oscilloscope test that shows AC-coupled analog inputs cannot pass a DC voltage. Explain in your own words what this property means for measuring subwoofer phase, and what running a signal through several AC-coupled stages does to the low-frequency phase response.Understand

    AC coupling acts as a high-pass filter on the signal path, blocking DC and rolling off the lowest frequencies. High-pass filters add positive phase shift to the frequencies near and below the cutoff. A single Yamaha Rio preamp introduces a small amount at the very bottom, but Sheets loops the signal through the Rio's input and output several times and shows the phase shift stacking up high enough to reach into the normal sub-frequency range (40 to 80 Hz). The implication is that the measurement chain itself corrupts the data the engineer is trying to read. Sheets's recommendation is to keep the signal path digital from the amp onward and only convert to analog at the speaker terminals.

  2. 2.The pink-noise test reveals that the current trace shows a sharp phase swing around 30 Hz (the impedance spike), but the laser trace of the actual cone motion is smooth through the same frequency. What does the difference between these two readings tell you about the relationship between the electrical and acoustic responses of a speaker, and what role does the Qts parameter play?Analyze

    The 30 Hz impedance spike comes from the driver's mechanical resonance, where the cone wants to move on its own most efficiently. At resonance the moving voice coil produces a reverse polarity current (back EMF) that pushes against the amplifier, which is why current flow drops and the phase trace swings sharply. The cone, however, is doing what it always does at resonance: moving smoothly and efficiently. The Qts parameter governs how much these two responses diverge. A Qts well below 0.707 means the electrical and physical forces are balanced enough that the resonance does not produce an acoustic bump; values above 0.707 produce an audible peak around the resonance frequency. Sheets's test driver has a Qts around 0.3, which is why the laser trace shows no anomaly where the current trace swings.

  3. 3.Sheets argues that the condenser mic is reading the acceleration of the driver, not its displacement, which produces a 180 degree phase offset relative to the cone's motion. Walk through the displacement-velocity-acceleration chain and explain how each derivative adds 90 degrees of phase shift. Why does the mic still show an in-phase spike at the very onset of the signal?Analyze

    Displacement is the cone's position. The first derivative of displacement is velocity, which is the cone's rate of change in position. Velocity peaks where displacement passes through zero, so the velocity trace is shifted 90 degrees ahead of the displacement trace. The second derivative is acceleration, the rate of change of velocity. Acceleration peaks where velocity is changing fastest, which is at the extremes of cone travel, putting it another 90 degrees ahead of velocity and a full 180 degrees ahead of displacement. Air pressure at the mic is governed by acceleration, not position, because pressure differentials form when the cone changes direction (suddenly creating a void on one side and pushing air on the other). The cone slowing down at the front of its travel creates a negative pressure that pulls the mic diaphragm outward, producing a negative voltage at the very moment the cone is at maximum positive displacement. The in-phase spike at the onset happens because the very first cone motion is not yet sinusoidal but a transient, and the air responds with an in-phase pressure pulse until steady-state oscillation takes over.

  4. 4.Sheets closes by listing the stages where phase shift can accumulate between a console output and a measurement-mic input: AC coupling, amplifier high-pass protection, driver mass and surround stiffness, the air's pressure-vs-displacement relationship, the mic's own high-pass filter, and possibly a reversed-polarity test capsule. Recommend a measurement workflow for a system tech trying to align mains and subs by ear plus a single measurement mic. Which of these phase contributors can be ignored, which must be corrected, and which must simply be remembered as offsets when reading the trace?Evaluate

    These contributors divide into fixed offsets and variable ones. Fixed offsets that can be remembered rather than corrected: the driver's 180 degree shift above resonance is intrinsic to that driver and shows up identically every time it is measured, and the condenser mic's 180 degree shift relative to the driver is similarly intrinsic. Both cancel out when comparing one mic trace against another at the same point in space, which is what alignment work requires. Contributors that must be corrected: AC coupling in the measurement chain (use a DC-coupled interface for absolute polarity tests, or stay digital from the amp onward), and a polarity-reversed mic capsule (verify polarity with a positive-pressure burst from the mouth or a battery test on the driver). Contributors that can be ignored when the work is comparative: the amplifier's high-pass protection circuit, since it appears equally on every channel of a transfer-function measurement. A reasonable workflow: confirm mic polarity once at setup, use a DC-coupled or fully digital chain when characterizing system phase below 100 Hz, and treat the driver-and-air offsets as expected baseline behavior that does not need to be tuned out, only understood when reading the trace.
