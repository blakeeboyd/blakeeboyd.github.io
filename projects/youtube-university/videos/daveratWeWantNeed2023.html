Do We Want & Need Audio Compressors?

Dave Rat · 21:43 · Reflective

Watch on YouTube ↗

Takeaway

The conventional view treats compression as a level tool for catching peaks or matching loudness. Rat argues compression's primary job in live sound is to fix the unnatural volume differentials that close miking introduces, like the way a singer moving 4 inches from the mic produces a 6 dB swing no listener would naturally experience. He extends the same logic across cymbals, singers, bass DIs, and digital consoles, and closes with a stove-spark demonstration that makes the proximity effect on direct vs. reverberant balance perceptible.

Questions

  1. 1.Rat says microphones "distort the audio in unnatural ways." Walk through his recorder-and-handling-noise example: what does the mic capture that a person in the room wouldn't hear at the same level, and why?Understand

    Rat's recorder captures handling noise from his fingers touching it, vibrations transmitted up the recorder body, and any motion he makes. A person standing next to him in the room hears those at much lower volume relative to his voice, because their ears are far from the source rather than in contact with it. The mic's close proximity amplifies sub-vocal sounds the brain normally ignores, turning handling noise into a major signal component.

  2. 2.Rat describes a conundrum: close miking captures unnatural volume differentials, while far miking captures too much room sound. Why is room sound harder to deal with than volume differentials, and how does compression turn close-miked signals into something closer to what a listener would naturally hear?Analyze

    Room sound is hard to remove because once captured, it's mixed inseparably with the desired signal. A gate can hide some of it during silences, but the moment the mic opens for the source, it's also open for the room. Volume differentials, by contrast, are a level problem (loud and quiet versions of the same signal), and compression specifically attacks level. It reduces gain on the loud passages and lifts the quiet ones to match.

  3. 3.Compare a bass DI to a bass mic. Why does Rat say the bass DI needs more compression than the bass mic, and what does this reveal about how amplifiers and speakers naturally compress dynamics before any console processing?Analyze

    A bass mic captures the speaker, which is already mechanically compressed by the amp driving the speaker's mechanical limits (the cone "squishes down" on the loudest notes). A bass DI taps the signal before the speaker, so it captures every variation in the player's attack, every subtle dynamics fluctuation. The DI sound is purer but more revealing of inconsistent playing; the mic sound is mellower because the speaker already did half the compression. To make a DI sound as forgiving as a mic, you compress harder, restoring the dynamic smoothing the speaker would have provided.

  4. 4.Rat argues that highly transient sound systems (Funktion-One, Danley) reproduce close-miked transients more dramatically than less-dynamic systems. Suppose you're mixing a rock show on a Funktion-One rig and a club show on a more dynamic-reduced reflex PA: how would you compress differently on each, and why?Apply

    On a Funktion-One rig, the system's high-transient reproduction will amplify every close-miked attack: a kick mic close to the head feels like a punch in the chest, every snare hit sounds enormous. To keep the mix from sounding spiky and unmusical, more compression is needed to tame those transients. On a less-dynamic reflex PA, the speaker design itself reduces transients, so the same mics need less compression because the system is already doing some of the smoothing.
