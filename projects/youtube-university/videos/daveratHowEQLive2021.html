How to EQ Live Sound Systems Tricks - Updated Version

Dave Rat · 15:09 · Applied

Live Sound / System Tuning, FOH Mixing, Monitor Engineering

eqfeedbacktroubleshooting

Watch on YouTube ↗

Takeaway

Rat lays out his framework for live-sound EQ: three distinct EQ locations, each responsible for a specific problem. Channel EQ fixes the mic-instrument combination, the system processor fixes the speaker-enclosure response, and the system/house graphic EQ fixes the speaker-to-venue interaction. He uses a high-quality pair of reference headphones as a constant reference across gigs: EQ each channel through the headphones until it sounds right, then A/B a familiar CD between the headphones and the PA to tune the system EQ. He closes with a caution about over-EQ on 1/3-octave graphics: cutting six bands removes roughly half the audible spectrum once you account for the actual bandwidth of each band, so reach for cut-and-sweep parametric EQs whenever you can.

Questions

  1. 1.Rat assigns each of the three EQ locations (channel EQ, system processor, system/house EQ) a specific problem to solve. Match each location to the problem and explain his reasoning. What goes wrong if you fix the room on the channel strips, or fix the microphone on the system EQ?Analyze

    Channel EQ = mic + instrument combination (one engineer's choice of mic on a particular guitar amp). System processor = speaker + enclosure (Meyer, JBL, etc. preset that compensates for the cabinet's response). System/house graphic = speaker system + venue (room reflections, listener position, ambient resonances). Fixing the room on the channel strips means every channel needs the same correction, and the moment you move venues those corrections become wrong: you've baked the room into your channel work. Fixing the mic on the system EQ means every mic gets the same correction, and you can't tune a vocal mic differently from a kick drum mic. You've lost individual control over mic-instrument problems.

  2. 2.Rat carries a familiar pair of high-quality headphones to every gig as a fixed reference. Walk through how he uses them to dial in both the channel EQs and the system EQ, and explain why the headphone-vs-PA A/B comparison can replace (or supplement) Smaart-style measurement.Apply

    For channel EQ: Rat sets up each channel and listens via the console's headphone output (which taps the signal pre-system EQ). He adjusts the channel EQ until the source sounds correct in the headphones, the same standard across every gig. For system EQ: he plays a familiar CD through the main system while simultaneously listening to the same CD through his headphones (plugged directly into the CD player). He A/Bs between the two until they sound tonally similar, then adjusts the system EQ to close the gap. The headphone-vs-PA comparison can replace or supplement Smaart because Smaart shows the magnitude response but not how it sounds to a human ear; comparing to a known good reference lets you tune by ear without analyzer software.

  3. 3.Rat warns that cutting six bands on a 1/3-octave graphic EQ removes roughly half the spectrum once you account for the actual bandwidth of each band. Explain his arithmetic, and describe one specific situation where this matters more than engineers usually realize.Analyze

    A 1/3-octave graphic EQ has 30 bands across the audio spectrum, each ostensibly a third of an octave wide. But the filter shape isn't a brick wall: each band is approximately three times wider than its labeled bandwidth at the half-cut point, so cutting one band affects roughly an octave of nearby frequencies. Cutting six bands at half-cut affects roughly 6 × 3 = 18 of the 30 bands at meaningful levels, with cumulative phase shifts and Q-interactions. The result is that you've cut more than half the spectrum even though only six sliders are down. Engineers tuning monitors notice this when they cut a lot of bands and the wedge starts sounding hollow or thin, not from the specific frequencies removed but from the cumulative bandwidth loss.

  4. 4.Apply Rat's framework to a monitor wedge that feeds back at 2 kHz on a vocal mic but sounds fine on a guitar. Where does he say the EQ work should happen, and what is the diagnostic principle (common-across-all-wedges vs. specific-to-one-mic) he uses to decide?Apply

    Rat's diagnostic principle: if the problem is common across all wedges and all instruments (every wedge feeds back at 2 kHz regardless of source), fix it on the wedge's graphic EQ; the problem lives in the wedge or the room. If the problem is specific to one mic on one wedge (vocal feeds back at 2 kHz but guitar is fine), fix it on the channel EQ for that mic; the problem lives in the mic-vocalist combination. For the vocal mic feeding back at 2 kHz while the guitar is fine, the fix is a narrow channel EQ cut at 2 kHz on the vocal channel, which leaves the wedge response intact for the guitar and other sources.
