Takeaway

Curtis covers EQ for system engineers, structured around Pat Brown's "paintbrush" workflow: start with one-octave banding and a wide brush, then move to one-third-octave, then one-sixth-octave, getting progressively narrower in scope. Curtis explains the distinction between speaker EQ (manufacturer-applied, flat target, anechoic) and system EQ (engineer-applied in the room, target-curve based) and then tackles the "phase boogeyman" with calm math: minimum-phase EQ does change phase, but the shift is calculable and small at typical move sizes (~30° for a +6 dB shelf), and equal-and-opposite filters sum to zero (complementary EQ). He closes by sorting common acoustic phenomena into EQ-solvable (low-mid buildup, with limits) and not-EQ-solvable (room modes, floor bounces, SBIR), with practical alternatives like moving subs against walls to eliminate boundary interference.

Questions

  1. 1.Curtis distinguishes speaker equalization from system equalization. What is each one for, who applies it, and how are the measurement environments different?Understand

    Speaker equalization is what a manufacturer applies to a single speaker box to make it produce a smooth response. It's measured in an anechoic environment (or very close, near-field), with the goal of a flat or designed magnitude response. Tools include passive crossover design plus DSP presets in the amp. System equalization is what the live engineer applies in the room. It puts already-equalized speakers together in a real venue, with measurement microphones in the audience area, targeting a target curve (not flat). Speaker EQ is the manufacturer's last step after careful design; system EQ is the engineer's last step after careful placement, aim, and time alignment.

  2. 2.Pat Brown's "paintbrush" workflow starts with one-octave banding and one-octave Q filters, then narrows to one-third-octave, then one-sixth-octave. Why progress in this order, and what goes wrong if an engineer starts with narrow Q filters and tries to hammer out every small deviation (the "whack-a-mole" approach)?Analyze

    Starting broad lets you fix the biggest tonality problems first with the fewest, widest filters. A 1-octave filter affects a whole region of the spectrum, so one or two filters can address major imbalances without introducing audible Q effects. Then you move tighter to address smaller deviations. The "whack-a-mole" approach (looking at a high-resolution trace and hammering every peak and dip with narrow filters) treats noise in the measurement (reflections, the microphone's specific position) as if it were the speaker's tonal balance. Different measurement positions show different "peaks and dips" at high resolution, so narrow EQ moves over-fit to one position and hurt others.

  3. 3.Curtis sorts acoustic phenomena into EQ-solvable and not-EQ-solvable. Walk through floor bounces and SBIR (speaker boundary interference response): why does EQ fail to fix these problems even though they appear as tonality issues in the measurement?Analyze

    Floor bounces are a single reflection arriving at the microphone delayed by some milliseconds (e.g., 9 ms for a head-height mic with a ceiling speaker). The reflection combines with the direct signal to create a comb filter (alternating peaks and dips at predictable frequencies). The cause is timing, not tonality: EQ would have to apply a frequency-specific cut at every comb dip, and the comb pattern moves as the listener moves. SBIR is similar: a sub near a back wall reflects off the wall, the reflection arrives delayed, and the combination creates frequency-dependent cancellation at the listener position. EQ can't fix the timing-based interference because the cancellation pattern is different at every audience seat. The fix is to move the speaker (closer to the boundary so the delay shrinks to nothing, or further from it so the reflection is masked by other absorption).

  4. 4.You're tuning a system and notice a deep dip at 56 Hz that's consistent across all your measurement positions. The dip also shows poor coherence (red line dips with the magnitude). What is your diagnostic process based on Curtis's framework, and what are your candidate causes and fixes?Apply

    The poor coherence at the dip frequency is the first diagnostic clue: coherence dropping means the measurement is unreliable at that frequency, often because of a destructive reflection at the mic's specific position. Candidate causes in order: (a) a room mode at 56 Hz that happens to coincide with this mic position (test by moving the mic and seeing if the dip moves); (b) a floor bounce (check the impulse response for a delayed spike and calculate the comb frequencies); (c) SBIR from a back wall reflection (check the sub's distance to nearby boundaries). Fixes: if room mode, accept the limitation since EQ won't help across the audience; if floor bounce, move the mic or the speaker; if SBIR, move the sub closer to or further from the boundary.
