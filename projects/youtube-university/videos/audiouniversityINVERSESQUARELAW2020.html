INVERSE SQUARE LAW of Sound

Takeaway

Audio University takes the everyday observation that sounds get quieter with distance and shows the geometry behind it. A sound source radiates a finite amount of energy outward as a sphere; as the sphere grows, that fixed energy spreads across a larger surface, so intensity falls. The numbers make it concrete: doubling the radius from 2 ft to 4 ft quadruples the surface area, leaving one quarter of the energy per square meter, which is the inverse square. The video then separates two related laws. The inverse square law describes intensity across the whole wavefront; the inverse distance law describes the pressure level our ears and microphones sample at a point, where doubling the distance costs 6 dB. It closes with the assumptions both laws depend on: an omnidirectional point source and a free-field with no reflecting boundaries.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Awareness that decibels are a logarithmic measure of level
  • Comfort with the idea that a sound wave carries energy outward from its source
  • No prior physics math required

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain why sound intensity drops with the square of distance using the spreading-sphere model
  • Distinguish the inverse square law (intensity) from the inverse distance law (pressure level)
  • Apply the 6 dB per doubling of distance rule to predict SPL at a new listening position
  • Identify the point-source and free-field assumptions that the laws depend on

Questions

  1. 1.The video explains the inverse square law with a sphere of acoustical energy that grows as the sound travels. In your own words, why does the energy per square meter drop to one quarter when the distance from the source only doubles? Use the surface-area relationship to make the case.Understand

    A strong response ties the drop to the surface area of a sphere, which grows with the square of the radius. The sound source puts out a fixed amount of energy, and that energy is spread across the surface of the sphere. When the radius doubles, the surface area increases by a factor of four (the radius term is squared in the area formula), so the same total energy is now divided among four times as much area. Energy per square meter therefore falls to one quarter. The squaring of the radius is the reason the law is "inverse square" rather than simply "inverse."

  2. 2.A microphone reads 100 dB SPL at 4 cm from a speaker. Using the inverse distance law, predict the reading at 8 cm, at 16 cm, and at 32 cm. State the rule you are applying and show each step.Apply

    The rule is the inverse distance law: each doubling of distance costs 6 dB of sound pressure level. Starting at 100 dB SPL at 4 cm: doubling to 8 cm gives 100 minus 6, or 94 dB SPL. Doubling again to 16 cm gives 94 minus 6, or 88 dB SPL. Doubling once more to 32 cm gives 88 minus 6, or 82 dB SPL. The key move is recognizing that each step is a halving of distance relative to the previous one, and that the 6 dB figure applies per doubling, not per meter.

  3. 3.The video distinguishes the inverse square law from the inverse distance law and says our ears and microphones measure the second one, not the first. Explain the difference between intensity across a wavefront and pressure level at a point, and why the video introduces a separate law for what a microphone samples.Analyze

    Intensity describes how much acoustic energy is distributed across the entire surface of the expanding wavefront, the quantity the inverse square law governs. Pressure level is what a single small sensor, an ear or a microphone diaphragm, registers at one point on that surface. The video introduces the inverse distance law because that is the law matching what a measurement device samples: it tracks pressure rather than total intensity, and it works out to a 6 dB loss per doubling of distance. Both laws describe the same physical spreading, but they answer different questions, one about the whole sphere and one about a point on it.

  4. 4.Both laws assume an omnidirectional point source in a free-field. Pick one of these assumptions and describe a recording or live-sound situation where it fails. Predict whether the actual level loss over distance would be more or less than the law alone would suggest, and why.Analyze

    For the free-field assumption: a recording made indoors, or a live PA in a room, has walls, a floor, and a ceiling that reflect sound back toward the listener. Those reflections add energy that the free-field model does not account for, so the level loss over distance is less than the inverse distance law predicts. The video notes that a nearby obstruction provides a reflection, causing less loss than the law predicts. For the point-source assumption: a line array or a large directional source does not radiate evenly in all directions, so close in, the level can drop much more slowly than 6 dB per doubling, approaching point-source behavior only at distance. A strong answer names the situation, names which assumption breaks, and reasons through the direction of the error.
