Takeaway

The technical follow-up to the basic loudspeaker video. Sheets and Austin (a tech director at a local church) work through the physics of subwoofer enclosures and sub arrays: how a vented cabinet works (port and driver 180° out of phase below tuning, in phase above), how port turbulence is controlled by curved walls and nozzle-shaped openings, how cardioid sub arrays are built from physical distance and digital delay, and how a two-driver cabinet with frequency-dependent delay can extend the cardioid effect across a much wider range. They close with the V-shaped slotted port that suppresses the harmonic phase shift plaguing older vented cabinets in the upper-mid range. The slow-motion port-airflow footage alone is worth the watch.

Questions

  1. 1.In a vented cabinet, the port and the driver are 180° out of phase below the tuning frequency and in phase above it. Explain in your own words why this happens, and what the audible effect is at, say, 20 Hz vs. 50 Hz in a cabinet tuned to 32 Hz.Analyze

    Below the tuning frequency, the air mass in the port is heavy relative to the driver's motion, so the port moves opposite to the driver (out of phase) and the two outputs cancel, which is why a 32 Hz-tuned cabinet produces almost no 20 Hz output. At the tuning frequency the port resonates with the air spring inside the cabinet, the phase relationship inverts, and above tuning the port moves in phase with the driver (both producing a positive pressure wave at the same time), so they sum constructively. At 50 Hz in a 32 Hz cabinet, port and driver are in phase and adding ~3-6 dB of output; at 20 Hz they cancel almost completely.

  2. 2.Sheets shows two different ways to build a directional subwoofer array: separating two subs by half-wavelength spacing with one polarity-flipped, vs. placing them at quarter-wavelength spacing with one delayed. Why does the second method produce a cardioid pattern instead of a figure-8?Analyze

    With half-wavelength spacing and one sub polarity-reversed, the two subs cancel on the axis between them (equidistant points) but sum symmetrically on either side: a figure-8 pattern, not cardioid. Quarter-wavelength spacing with a quarter-cycle digital delay shifts the cancellation pattern: in one direction the delayed sub arrives exactly out of phase with the front sub (cancellation behind), in the other direction they arrive together (summation in front). The delay breaks the symmetry that pure spacing produces, producing a one-sided null: cardioid.

  3. 3.What is the fundamental limitation of cardioid sub arrays that Sheets identifies, and how does the two-driver cabinet with frequency-dependent delay address it?Analyze

    Cardioid sub arrays require a specific physical distance and delay tuned to one frequency; above and below that frequency, the phase relationship drifts and the cardioid pattern degrades. The two-driver cabinet exploits the fact that the port handles the lowest-frequency output and the driver handles slightly higher output, so the effective acoustic distance between front and rear sources is larger at low frequencies (port-dominant) and smaller at higher frequencies (driver-dominant). Combined with frequency-dependent digital delay, each frequency band sees roughly the right combination of distance and delay for the cardioid effect to hold over a much wider range.

  4. 4.Older vented cabinets have a harmonic above the tuning frequency that causes phase cancellations in the upper-mid range. What did the V-shaped slotted port do to suppress this, and why does it matter for full-range speakers that have to cover from the subs up to the horn?Analyze

    Older vented cabinets have a second resonance above the tuning frequency where the port produces audible output that is 180° out of phase with the driver, producing comb-filter cancellations in the upper midrange. The V-shaped notch cut into the internal port wall interrupts the air column that would otherwise form the standing wave for this harmonic. The phase response stays nearly linear through a much higher frequency range, so the cabinet sums coherently with the next driver in the system (the horn or midrange) rather than fighting it. This matters because full-range cabinets must cover from low bass up to where the horn takes over; any cancellation in that handoff band shows up as midrange smear.
