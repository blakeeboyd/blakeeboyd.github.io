Takeaway

Audio University answers a familiar complaint (buried vocals at the club show) by pointing past the engineer to stage volume. The argument: in a small room the drums are already too loud before any mic goes up, so the PA should reinforce the quiet sources rather than make everything louder. That reframing drives a chain of practical decisions. Skip drum overheads because the cymbals do not need help; mic the kick and maybe the snare because those are what the room cannot hear. Start soundcheck with the vocal mics, because they are the most important channel and the most feedback-prone, so their maximum gain before feedback sets the ceiling under which everything else has to fit. The back half covers cymbal bleed into vocal mics pushed close to their feedback threshold, why noise gates false-trigger on stage, drum and amp placement, and the case for in-ear monitors as a way to stop the monitor-level arms race that drives stage volume up.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with the basic live signal chain: stage mic to console to PA
  • Awareness that microphone feedback limits how loud a mic can get
  • Some exposure to a typical band stage layout (drums, amps, vocal mics)

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain the difference between sound reinforcement and studio capture and why it changes miking decisions in a small room
  • Apply the question 'what part of the kit can I not hear' to decide which drum mics to use
  • Analyze why starting soundcheck with the vocal mics produces a more workable mix than starting with the kick
  • Evaluate the trade-offs of in-ear monitors against floor wedges for controlling stage volume
