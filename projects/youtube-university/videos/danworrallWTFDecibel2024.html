WTF is a Decibel?

Takeaway

Worrall tackles the most fundamental measurement in audio engineering. The key reframing is that decibels work the same way as musical pitch, since both are logarithmic. Adding 12 semitones doubles the frequency; adding 6 dB doubles the amplitude. Subtracting works the inverse way. So decibels are ratios, not absolute quantities, which is why "this concert is 120 dB" doesn't actually mean anything without a reference like dBFS, dBu, dBV, or dB SPL. He closes with a glass-of-water analogy that makes the math vivid: attenuating by 100 dB doesn't leave you with 16.6% of the water, it leaves you with about 0.001%.

Questions

  1. 1.Worrall says decibels work the same way as musical pitch: both are logarithmic scales. Walk through the analogy. What does +12 semitones do to frequency, and what does +6 dB do to amplitude? Why is this comparison helpful for understanding why -6 dB doesn't subtract a fixed amount?Understand

    +12 semitones doubles the frequency (an octave up); -12 semitones halves it. +6 dB doubles the amplitude; -6 dB halves it. Both are logarithmic scales because what feels like an even step to our ear or our intuition (one octave up, 6 dB louder) corresponds to a multiplicative change, not an additive one. The analogy explains why -6 dB doesn't subtract a fixed amount: it always halves whatever amplitude is currently there, the same way -12 semitones always halves whatever frequency is currently there.

  2. 2.A decibel "is actually a ratio" and "doesn't actually mean anything" without a reference. Explain what dBFS, dBu, dBV, and dB SPL each reference, and what the difference would be between calling a concert "120 dB" vs. "120 dB SPL."Understand

    dBFS (decibels full scale): referenced to 0 dBFS, the maximum digital level; everything else is negative. dBu and dBV: referenced to specific analog voltages (0.775 V and 1 V respectively), both for line-level signals. dB SPL: referenced to the threshold of human hearing (20 µPa); the loudness scale used to describe concert SPL, traffic noise, etc. So "120 dB" alone is meaningless because you don't know what's being doubled relative to what. "120 dB SPL" means a sound 120 dB louder than the threshold of hearing, which is concert-loud. "120 dBFS" doesn't make sense because full scale is the maximum; you can't go louder than zero.

  3. 3.Worrall says the commenter's glass-of-water analogy got the math backwards: if there are 120 dB of water in a glass and you remove 100 dB, you don't have 16.6% left, you have about 0.001%. Reconstruct the arithmetic and explain why this is so much smaller than naive subtraction would suggest.Apply

    Every 6 dB of attenuation halves the amplitude. 100 dB of attenuation is roughly 100/6 ≈ 16.7 halvings, so the amplitude is multiplied by (1/2)^16.7 ≈ 1/100,000 ≈ 0.001%. The naive subtraction (120 - 100 = 20, 20/120 ≈ 16.6%) treats decibels as linear units like pounds of water, but decibels are logarithmic ratios. Each 6 dB removed is half of whatever is currently in the glass, not a fixed amount.

  4. 4.Worrall says digital audio formats technically have infinite dynamic range because they can do total silence (a string of zeros), and zero is "minus infinity" dB rather than any specific dB value. Why is total silence not the same as -120 dB or -140 dB? How does this connect to why dither matters near the noise floor of a fixed-bit format?Analyze

    A string of zero samples has no amplitude at all: it's true silence, which mathematically corresponds to negative infinity in dB (the log of zero is -∞). Any specific dB value (-120, -140) is some small but non-zero amplitude. Floating-point digital audio can express truly zero amplitude exactly; fixed-bit formats can't represent values below their quantization step, but they can store zero. Dither matters at the bottom of a fixed-bit range because signals get rounded to the nearest quantization step, adding harmonic distortion correlated with the signal. Dither adds a small random noise that decorrelates the rounding errors, trading a small constant noise for the elimination of audible distortion at the noise floor.
