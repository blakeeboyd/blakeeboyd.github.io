Takeaway

Curtis takes the question every small-format engineer faces, two speakers on sticks and an audience to cover, and replaces guesswork with arithmetic. He frames coverage through a lighting-designer analogy: a speaker is a flashlight, and the job is to point its beam at people. The original move is borrowing Bob McCarthy's forward aspect ratio (FAR) and reducing it to fourth-grade math. Audience FAR is depth divided by width; the spreadsheet then returns the coverage angle of the speaker that would fit that shape. When the audience is wider than it is deep, no single speaker can cover it, so the method says "subdivide and conquer": split the room into zones and hand each to one speaker. Curtis runs the calculation both directions, from audience shape to required speaker, and from a fixed speaker (the QSC K12 at 75 degrees) back to the audience zone it can serve, using the speaker FAR formula $\text{FAR} = \frac{1}{\sin(\theta/2)}$. He ends on placement and aim: put each speaker in the middle of its zone and shoot through the middle of the middle.

Questions

  1. 1.Curtis calls nominal coverage angle "the number one spec I look for on any speaker." Explain what nominal coverage angle describes, what "axisymmetric" means using the K12 as the example, and why a line-array box like the RCF HDL 6-A has two very different coverage numbers.Understand

    Nominal coverage angle is the angle within which the speaker maintains its intended high-frequency level before the response drops off, marking the edges of the usable beam. "Axisymmetric" means the horizontal and vertical coverage are equal, so the K12's 75-degree pattern is a cone, the same angle whichever way you rotate it. A line-array box is built to do the opposite: the RCF HDL 6-A is roughly 100 degrees horizontally but only about 10 degrees vertically, because line-array elements are meant to be stacked and splayed so the array as a whole controls vertical coverage. The single coverage number assumes a symmetric device and breaks down for any speaker designed for asymmetric horizontal and vertical control.

  2. 2.Curtis's audience FAR is depth divided by width. For the show in the video the audience is 50 feet deep by 64 feet wide. Calculate the audience FAR, explain why the spreadsheet returns "subdivide and conquer" instead of a coverage angle, and show what the FAR becomes once the room is split into a left and a right zone.Apply

    Audience FAR is $50 \div 64 \approx 0.78$. The spreadsheet returns "subdivide and conquer" because the audience is wider than it is deep: no single speaker can cover a shape with a FAR below 1. Splitting the room into a left and a right zone halves the width each speaker must cover, so the FAR becomes $50 \div 32 \approx 1.56$, which the method maps to roughly an 80-degree speaker for each side. Credit responses that note the left-right split sacrifices a small amount of stereo imaging for even coverage, a compromise Curtis treats as worthwhile in live sound.

  3. 3.The speaker FAR formula is $\text{FAR} = \frac{1}{\sin(\theta/2)}$, where $\theta$ is the nominal coverage angle. Use it to find the FAR of a 100-degree speaker, then describe an audience zone (in depth-to-width terms) that this speaker would cover well, and one it would not.Apply

    For a 100-degree speaker, $\theta/2 = 50°$, $\sin(50°) \approx 0.766$, so $\text{FAR} = 1 / 0.766 \approx 1.31$. (Curtis's spreadsheet reports about 1.53 for 100 degrees; either value is acceptable if the substitution into the formula is shown correctly, and noting the discrepancy is a sign of careful work.) A zone with a depth-to-width ratio near the speaker's FAR is a good match: a deep, relatively narrow zone. An audience much wider than it is deep, with a FAR well below the speaker's number, is a poor match and signals the need to subdivide. The number ties back to the matchmaking idea: the speaker FAR and the audience FAR should be close.

  4. 4.Curtis argues that if you must err, a slightly too-wide speaker beats a slightly too-narrow one. Walk through the trade-offs he names on each side, and connect the too-narrow failure mode back to the "under coverage" weather map he shows with the two 10-degree mains.Analyze

    Too wide: more energy overlaps between adjacent speakers, which can cause comb filtering, and more sound sprays onto walls, a cost if the system is already short on SPL. Too narrow: parts of the audience fall entirely outside the beam and get little usable level, which Curtis shows as the dark, low-intelligibility center of the under-coverage weather map produced by two 10-degree mains. Curtis prefers the wider error because its failure is degraded uniformity, while the narrow error's failure is people who cannot hear the show. A good answer will frame it as choosing the failure mode you can live with, and note that wider also leaves room to recover with aim and placement.
