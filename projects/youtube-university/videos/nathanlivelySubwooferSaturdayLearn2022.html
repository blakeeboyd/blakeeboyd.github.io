Subwoofer Saturday - Learn the Basics of Subwoofer Arrays in Live Sound

Takeaway

Lively opens with a failure story: he designed a subwoofer array for a Walmart corporate picnic that worked on paper, but the front-of-house position had to move off-axis, and the bass disappeared because the array shape changed over frequency. He teaches the left-hand rule for predicting which direction a sub array will narrow, walks through the four main directional configurations (cardioid, end-fire, gradient, broadside), defends center clusters over left-right subs for even coverage, and lands on his two principles of subwoofer alignment: do the preparation work in the warehouse, then equalize the distance offset between subs and mains with delay. The long Q&A tail covers air absorption, the 10 dB combing zone where main and sub interact, coupled vs uncoupled spacing, and why he avoids aux-fed subs.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with phase, polarity, and basic comb filtering
  • Awareness that subwoofers are usually deployed as arrays, not as a single box
  • Comfort with the idea that two coherent sources interfere in predictable ways

By the end you'll be able to

  • Use the left-hand rule to predict the narrowing axis of any sub array
  • Distinguish cardioid, end-fire, gradient, and broadside configurations by how they cancel rearward energy
  • Apply Lively's two principles of alignment (prep, then delay) to a real deployment
  • Evaluate a center-cluster vs left-right sub decision against the goal of even coverage

Questions

  1. 1.Lively opens with a Walmart picnic story where the subwoofer array he designed sounded right on axis but lost bass at the off-axis front-of-house position. In your own words, explain what went wrong and why he describes the array as having "changed shape over frequency."Understand

    First, the array's coverage pattern depends on frequency: the narrow axis shifts as frequency changes, so the covered area follows. Second, his array narrowed too aggressively at high frequencies (the upper part of the sub range), so when the FOH position shifted off the central axis, the higher sub frequencies fell off faster than the lower ones, producing the perception of "no bass." Third, the mismatch between the sub array's coverage shape and the mains' coverage shape created variance that changed tonality as listeners moved across the audience, which is the alignment problem the rest of the workshop addresses. The lesson: design the sub array's coverage to match the mains across the audience, not just to look symmetrical on paper.

  2. 2.Use Lively's left-hand rule to predict the narrowing axis for each of these arrays: (a) four subs in a horizontal line on the ground across the front of the stage, (b) four subs in a vertical column flown stage-left, (c) a cardioid array with three subs front and one sub rear-firing. State which direction each array narrows and which direction it stays wide.Apply

    (a) Horizontal line across the front of the stage: narrows along the horizontal axis (sound concentrates left-right of center, falls off above and below). This is why ground-stacked horizontal lines beam side-to-side. (b) Vertical column flown stage-left: narrows along the vertical axis (sound concentrates up and down at the column, falls off in front of and behind it). This is the line-array geometry applied to subs. (c) Cardioid array, three front and one rear-firing: the cardioid behavior cancels rearward energy. The narrowing axis depends on the line you draw through the cabinets; if they are stacked vertically the array narrows vertically, but the cardioid cancellation also produces a rear null perpendicular to the front-rear axis. The left-hand rule predicts the beam-narrowing axis; cardioid behavior is a separate cancellation pattern overlaid on it.

  3. 3.Lively's two principles of alignment are preparation and delay. He says the battle of alignment is won in the warehouse, not in the field. Distinguish what gets settled in the warehouse from what gets settled in the field, and explain why he insists on this order.Analyze

    Preparation (warehouse work): picking the right cabinets, building physical templates for known array geometries, programming presets in the speaker processor, measuring the cabinet's response so you know its phase trace, deciding which array configuration matches the venue shape, and entering known delay values into the processor before the truck rolls. Delay (field work): equalizing the time-of-arrival difference between subs and mains at the listener with delay, fine-tuning by walking the room with a transfer-function tool or measurement mic. Lively insists on this order because the delay step is fast and mechanical once the array is right, but it is impossible to correct a wrong array choice with delay alone. If the array is wrong, the field looks like Walmart: you arrive, the geometry has shifted, and you have no template to fall back on. The "every battle is won before it is fought" frame is literal here: preparation done, the field work is short and the result is repeatable.

  4. 4.Lively states a strong preference for center-cluster subs over a left-right split, and frames it as a personal preference. Build a case for or against splitting subs left-right at a small festival stage where the audience runs wide and shallow. Use his framework of even coverage, comb filtering between coherent sources, and the goal you set for the audience.Evaluate

    A strong response weighs three things. For splitting: matches the mains' stereo image, gives some low-end imaging information, and for a wide, shallow audience the two sub sources can cover the full width without combing if audience depth is sufficient for path-length differences to become small relative to wavelength. Against splitting: two coherent sub sources separated by a wide stage will produce strong comb filtering along the center plane of the audience (the place where most paying listeners sit), with predictable nulls that do not move because both sources play the same signal. Center-cluster subs collapse the stereo problem into a single source and let the engineer aim the cluster's narrowing axis along the deep axis of the audience for even bass coverage. A defensible answer picks one position, names the trade explicitly (stereo imaging in the lows is mostly inaudible below ~120 Hz, so the cost of going mono in the subs is small), and connects to the goal stated at the top of Lively's workshop: even coverage across the audience.
