Do you understand the phase graph?

Alpha Sound · 08:53 · Applied

Live Sound / System Tuning, Signal Processing, Filter Theory · Audio Theory / System Tuning, Signal Processing, Filter Theory

phaseeq

Watch on YouTube ↗

Takeaway

Sheets opens with a pop quiz: when the phase trace slopes downward, are the low frequencies actually behind the high frequencies, or not? The answer turns out to be "it depends on what you're measuring." He builds the case in three stages. First, he adds delay to test signals through linear-phase filters and shows that delay does shift each band's phase trace downward in proportion to the delay amount, while unwrapping reveals how the graph keeps the line continuous even when the underlying frequencies are not actually offset from one another. Second, he switches to an oscilloscope view with stacked 20 Hz and 160 Hz tones and demonstrates how each frequency moves independently in time when delayed. Third, he introduces a minimum-phase EQ filter and shows on a single-sample impulse and a single-cycle 120 Hz waveform that the filter reshapes the waveform without ever pushing the start of the sound earlier than it began. The video closes on the diagnostic point: a downward phase slope on a speaker measurement may indicate physical time arrival differences (cone mass, miscalibrated processing), while the same slope from a minimum-phase EQ is pure waveform reshaping. Global delay fixes the first.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with reading a magnitude trace and a phase trace on a measurement display
  • Basic understanding of delay alignment for stacked speakers or processed signals
  • Awareness that filters can be either minimum-phase or linear-phase

By the end you'll be able to

  • Read a downward-sloping phase trace as the visual signature of a time delay
  • Distinguish wrapped from unwrapped phase display and explain why the line stays continuous
  • Explain why a minimum-phase EQ produces phase shift without delaying the start of the sound
  • Decide whether a phase anomaly points to delay alignment or to filter behavior, based on what is being measured

Questions

  1. 1.Sheets uses three linear-phase EQ channel strips with low, mid, and high filters and adds delay to each in turn. Describe what happens to the phase trace when only the mid-range is delayed, and explain why the trace stays continuous instead of jumping back up to zero in the high band.Understand

    The trace remains at zero through the low band, slopes downward across the mid band where the delay was added, and then continuing at the lowered value through the high band rather than returning to zero. The continuity comes from how the unwrapped phase display works: each frequency only knows about its immediate neighbors, so once the trace drops in the mid band, the high band has no reason to climb back up because no delay was added to it relative to the band just below it. The visual continuity is a property of the graph, not of the actual time arrival of those frequencies.

  2. 2.Sheets says each frequency on the phase graph "is only thinking about the one directly on either side of it." Use the oscilloscope segment (20 Hz stacked on 160 Hz) to explain what he means, and why this local-only behavior is what produces the apparently global downward slope when delay is added.Analyze

    Look for use of the oscilloscope segment to show that delaying just 20 Hz moves the 20 Hz trace in time while the 160 Hz trace stays put, and vice versa. Frequencies are independent in time. The phase graph then shows a slope because, looking at any single point, the local rate of change in phase reflects how the neighboring frequencies are arranged. A continuous slope across a band means each frequency in that band is shifted slightly more than the one below it, but globally the band could be in any time relationship to bands outside it. The "local-only" view explains why an unwrapped slope can extend indefinitely without implying that the lowest frequency is actually delayed by the cumulative phase amount.

  3. 3.Compare what the regular EQ does to the single-sample impulse and the single-cycle 120 Hz waveform with what a linear-phase EQ doing the same filter does to those signals. What is visibly different about how each filter type handles the start of the sound, and what does Sheets mean by "digital look-ahead"?Analyze

    The regular (minimum-phase) EQ reshapes the single-cycle 120 Hz waveform into what Sheets calls an "endless staircase" effect: the waveform looks like it is moving forward in time as amplitude decreases, but the start of the sound never moves earlier than the original tick. Post-ringing trails after the impulse. The linear-phase EQ produces ringing both before and after the impulse, symmetrical around the original start time, while keeping the main waveform's phase intact as it cuts amplitude. "Digital look-ahead" means the linear-phase filter delays the whole signal by enough samples that it can analyze a window of future audio before producing each output sample, which is what allows it to produce pre-ringing without violating causality. The cost is latency.

  4. 4.Sheets closes by arguing that interpreting a downward phase slope depends on whether the measurement is of a speaker or of a filter. Defend or complicate this position: when is "add global delay" the right corrective move, and when would it make things worse?Evaluate

    There are two cases to distinguish. If the measurement is of a speaker (RTA mic in front of a stacked system, for example), a downward phase slope may indicate actual time arrival differences from cone mass, processor latency, or miscalibrated delay lines: adding global delay to the reference or to specific bands is the correct correction. If the measurement is of a processing unit that uses minimum-phase EQ, the same slope is pure waveform reshaping: all frequencies still start at the same instant, and global delay would only add latency without changing the relative timing of any frequency. The harder cases sit in between: a system measurement that already includes processing, where part of the slope is real arrival difference and part is filter behavior. The diagnostic move is to know the signal chain and isolate where each contribution comes from before reaching for the delay knob.
