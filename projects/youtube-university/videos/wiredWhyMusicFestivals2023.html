Takeaway

A brief WIRED explainer with Dave Rat (who has provided audio for Coachella since 2001) on how the vertical line array transformed music festivals. The video walks through point-source vs. line-array configurations, the comb-filtering pattern of a point-source field (beamy hot spot in the middle, fades on the sides), the L-Acoustics aiming software that made angled-box line arrays practical to deploy in the field, and a then-and-now photo tour of Coachella that traces the transition from 90 four-foot boxes per side to slim line arrays to the K1 system with 24-box hangs. Rat closes with his view that the goal is to "make the sound system invisible."

Questions

  1. 1.Rat contrasts point-source and line-array configurations. Describe what each looks like physically (how the speakers are stacked or distributed) and what audible problem the line array is solving. Why does Rat say line arrays "pile the problems up where we hear them least"?Analyze

    Point source: speakers stacked or scattered, each acting as an independent source. Listeners hear sound waves from each speaker arriving at different times, creating comb-filtering interference that's audible as a beamy hot spot in the middle and fades on the sides. Line array: speakers vertically stacked, each separated by precise angles. The speakers couple together horizontally (sound waves combine smoothly side-to-side) but interfere destructively vertically (sound cuts off sharply above and below the array's aim). "Piles the problems up where we hear them least" means the unavoidable comb-filter cancellations end up in directions the audience doesn't occupy (the sky, the floor), leaving the audience plane smooth.

  2. 2.Walk through Rat's comb-filtering demonstration: what does the audience hear in a point-source system as they move across a field, and why does a vertical line array deliver a smoother experience across the horizontal coverage area?Analyze

    In a point-source system, an audience member walking across the field hears the sound dramatically change: where multiple speakers' arrival times happen to be in phase at a given frequency, the level is high (a "hot spot"); where they're out of phase, the level drops (a "dead zone"). The result is uneven loudness and tonal variation across the field. A vertical line array couples the speakers together in the horizontal direction so the entire audience plane receives nearly identical sound: same level, same EQ, same imaging. The smoothness comes from the array behaving like a single tall source rather than competing point sources.

  3. 3.What did L-Acoustics' aiming software change about the practice of designing arrays in the field? Before the software, Rat says they "just drew a bunch of X's" and eyeballed it. After, they could determine the angles "down to a fraction of a degree." What does this increase in precision let designers do that they couldn't before?Analyze

    Before the software, line-array deployment was empirical: hang the boxes, listen, adjust angles by eye. The result was generally bad: wrong angles meant wrong coverage, with audience members in some spots getting terrible sound. The software lets engineers compute the exact angle between each box pair given the audience geometry, accounting for distance and elevation. With precise angles, engineers can aim energy at specific zones (the audience), have it fall off in unwanted directions (above the audience, into neighboring stages), and steer sound to specific seating sections at full SPL. This precision enables festival coverage that targets one stage's audience without bleeding into the next stage.

  4. 4.Rat closes by saying line arrays let engineers "make the sound system invisible." What does he mean, and how is this aesthetic goal connected to the technical evolution from 90-box rigs to slim line arrays that the photo tour walks through?Evaluate

    "Invisible" means the audience hears the music without being aware of the system delivering it: no visible speakers (modern slim line arrays disappear into the stage), no audible reminders that the sound is amplified (no obvious comb filtering, no obvious off-axis differences across the audience). The aesthetic goal matches the technical evolution: 90-box rigs were visible, heavy, and produced uneven coverage with audible artifacts of their physical arrangement. Slim line arrays with computed aiming produce more even coverage from a smaller visual footprint. Each technical step (point source → flying line arrays → K1 with 24-box hangs and precise aiming) moves closer to a system that delivers the sound without announcing its presence, making the artistic experience feel direct rather than mediated.
