Takeaway

Evan McElhinney runs monitors for Styx, and this is the rare extended look at the monitor world rather than front of house: the engineer whose whole job is what the band hears on stage. His organizing principle is removing variables. He walks through a daily in-ear cleaning routine and a coupling-mic measurement that checks driver health rather than mix EQ, identical DiGiCo SD12 consoles at FOH and monitors that share head-amp gain with automatic tracking, a snapshot-driven show with control groups, mirrored fader layers and a one-to-one backup layout that keep the show running through a hardware failure, daily RF coordination at a noisy fairground, and a standardized cable-and-power discipline that ends on a small crusade against crossed cam-lock connections. The throughline is consistency as a craft: build the rig so the same show happens every night and nothing surprises the band.

Questions

  1. 1.McElhinney runs monitors, not front of house. In your own words, what is a monitor engineer responsible for, and why does he build a separate independent mix for each performer instead of sending everyone one shared mix?Understand

    The monitor engineer owns what the musicians hear on stage: the in-ear and wedge feeds. Each performer hears the band from a different physical and musical position, so a singer, a drummer, and a bass player each need a different balance to perform well. McElhinney runs 12 in-ear mixes plus wedge and hardwired feeds, all radically different from one another. The monitor mix exists for the player's performance, which is why it is built per person.

  2. 2.Every day McElhinney couples a microphone to each in-ear and reads its trace in Smaart, but he says you should not use this to make EQ decisions for the monitors. What is the measurement actually telling him (consider the phase trace and the comparison against a trace captured two months ago), and why is "driver health, not mix EQ" the right way to read it?Analyze

    Look for the recognition that the trace is a consistency check. By coupling the same reference signal through each earpiece and comparing today's trace against one captured two months ago when the ears were new, McElhinney is watching for drift: a phase trace that flipped would point to a wiring or polarity fault, and a frequency response that suddenly slopes off (his example is a dead low-frequency driver dropping out around 125 Hz) means a failed driver. A strong answer explains why this is a driver-health check: the in-ear's response measured in a coupler differs from what the musician hears in their own ear, so EQing to flatten the screen would chase an artifact. The measurement confirms the hardware still behaves the way it did when new.

  3. 3.FOH and monitors share one stage rack, and McElhinney controls the head-amp gain while the FOH engineer's console "tracks" it with a digital trim. Put yourself at the monitor console mid-show: you need 1 dB more on a channel and you nudge the head amp. Walk through what happens on both consoles, and explain why this setup lets you grab gain without disrupting the front-of-house mix.Apply

    At the monitor desk, raising the shared head amp by 1 dB sends 1 dB more on that channel to every console fed by the stage rack, front of house included. Gain tracking means the FOH console immediately applies a compensating digital trim of negative 1 dB in tenth-of-a-dB steps, so the audience mix stays where the FOH engineer left it while your monitor mix keeps the gain you wanted. A good response separates the two layers: the analog head amp is shared and physical, so a change there is global, but each console's digital trim is local, so tracking lets the two engineers share one preamp without fighting over it. Credit answers that note this removes any need to interrupt the FOH engineer to coordinate a gain change mid-show.

  4. 4.McElhinney puts the kick drums on a VCA and ducks them eight to ten dB at big song endings to protect the singers' ears, framing it as helping the band "play in their 70s." Evaluate this practice: is hearing conservation part of a monitor engineer's job, and what trade-offs does ducking the kicks introduce for the mix and for the performers who still want that energy?Evaluate

    A solid answer takes a position and defends it. For hearing conservation being part of the job: the monitor engineer controls the levels going into the performers' ears, so they are the person best placed to prevent cumulative damage, and McElhinney frames it as career longevity, keeping players able to perform into their 70s. The trade-offs: ducking the kicks at big endings pulls some of the physical impact the players feel at exactly the climactic moment, and McElhinney himself does not duck the drummer (who needs the kick to play) or the bass player (who wants it), so the practice is selective rather than absolute. The strongest responses weigh the protection against the loss of feel and note that the call is made per performer.
