EQ Doesn't Cause Phase Shift...

Dan Worrall · 04:22 · Applied

Audio Theory / Signal Processing, Filter Theory

eqphase

Watch on YouTube ↗

Takeaway

Worrall demonstrates in Reaktor that every standard EQ filter shape (low-pass, high-pass, low shelf, high shelf, bell, notch) can be derived from an all-pass filter (which is flat in frequency but introduces phase shift) plus simple arithmetic. Add the all-pass to the dry signal and you get a low-pass. Subtract it (or invert and add) and you get a high-pass. Scale and add and you get a shelf with adjustable gain. Wire two all-passes in series and you get a bell. The punchline reverses the usual question: EQ doesn't cause phase shift. Phase shift causes EQ. The real question is how an all-pass filter selectively shifts the phase of certain frequencies while leaving the amplitude completely flat, which Worrall leaves as an open puzzle.

Questions

  1. 1.Worrall starts with an all-pass filter that has a totally flat frequency response but causes increasing phase shift across the spectrum. Why does this filter sound inaudible on its own, and what is it actually doing to the signal?Understand

    The all-pass has a totally flat frequency response: every frequency comes out at the same level it went in. The only thing it changes is the phase relationship between frequencies, and a single signal in isolation reveals nothing about its phase (because phase is relative). You can only perceive the all-pass's effect when you sum its output with the dry signal or compare it to another phase reference. By itself, it sounds identical to the input.

  2. 2.Worrall builds a low-pass, a high-pass, a high shelf, and a bell out of nothing more than an all-pass filter plus addition or subtraction. Walk through one of these (say, the high shelf) and explain how parallel summing with a phase-shifted copy produces a frequency-selective gain change.Analyze

    An all-pass filter shifts phase increasingly across the cutoff, ending at 180° in the high end. Subtract the all-pass from the dry signal: at low frequencies (no phase shift) the subtraction cancels everything; at high frequencies (180° shift, i.e., inverted) the subtraction adds to the dry signal, doubling the high end. Result: a high-pass shape. Scale that high-pass by a gain knob and add it back to the dry signal: you've reinforced the high frequencies without changing the lows: a high shelf with adjustable gain. The arithmetic chain (phase-shifted copy → subtract for high-pass → scale → add back) builds the shelf out of nothing but phase shift and addition.

  3. 3.The video's punchline is that "EQ doesn't cause phase shift. Phase shift causes EQ." What does Worrall mean by this, and why is it not just a semantic flip? How does this reframing change the way an engineer should think about the unavoidable phase shifts that come with EQ boosts and cuts?Analyze

    The reframing changes what an engineer is actually doing when they boost or cut. EQ filters are typically taught as frequency-domain shapes: "this filter cuts 200 Hz by 6 dB." But the underlying mechanism is phase manipulation: the filter shifts the phases of certain frequencies in a way that, when summed back with the dry signal, produces the desired amplitude change. Understanding this explains why minimum-phase EQ always introduces phase shift (it's not a side effect; it's the mechanism), why linear-phase EQ trades that for pre-ringing, and why some "transparent" EQs sound different from others despite identical frequency response.

  4. 4.To get a bell-shaped boost in the mid-range, Worrall wires two all-passes in series so that the high frequencies get shifted 360° back into phase. Why does it take two all-passes rather than one to make a bell, and what would happen if you tried to build a bell from just a single all-pass and an addition module?Analyze

    A single all-pass produces 180° of total phase shift across the spectrum (from 0° at low frequencies to 180° at high). To make a bell, you need to shift only the midrange and leave both ends untouched. Two all-passes in series at the bell's center frequency produce 360° of total shift (returning the high frequencies to in-phase) but pass through 180° at the center, so subtracting that signal from the dry creates a cancellation only at the center frequency (a notch), and adding it produces a bell boost. A single all-pass can't make a bell because it only inverts one end of the spectrum, not the middle.
