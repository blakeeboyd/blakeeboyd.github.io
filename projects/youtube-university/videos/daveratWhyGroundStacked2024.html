Why Ground Stacked PA Sucks

Dave Rat · 06:31 · Applied

Live Sound / System Design, Acoustics · Audio Theory / System Design, Acoustics

loudspeakersroom-acoustics

Watch on YouTube ↗

Takeaway

Rat argues that flying the PA is partly an acoustics problem and not only a sightline problem. The opening case is geometric: a flown rig hangs 20 to 30 feet up, so the nearest listener can be 30 feet away and the farthest 100 feet, roughly a 3:1 ratio, while a ground stack puts the closest person 5 feet away and the farthest 100, a 20:1 ratio that produces uneven coverage and ear-level horn blast for the front rows. The original move in this video is the second case: Rat sets up a tabletop demonstration with a row of unlit candles standing in for an audience, lights them to simulate body heat (~90°F radiating from people into a 65 to 70°F room), and uses a mic switcher and pink noise to A/B a ground-stacked speaker against a flown one over the same heated plane. The flown system reaches the listening mic clearly; the ground-stacked system audibly loses energy as the sound refracts upward through the thermal layer. The skipping-stone analogy frames it: low angle of incidence skips off the heated layer, steep angle of incidence penetrates it. He closes with the outdoor-venue corollary, where sun-heated ground extends a system's throw during the day and shortens it as the ground cools, an effect best avoided by deploying multiple delay clusters rather than one over-aimed long throw.

Before you watch

Prerequisites

  • Familiar with basic live PA configurations (mains, fills, ground stack vs. flown)
  • Awareness that temperature affects the speed of sound
  • Some exposure to listening to a stereo PA across an audience

By the end you'll be able to

  • Distinguish geometric and acoustic reasons for flying a PA above the audience
  • Explain how audience body heat creates a refractive thermal layer above the floor
  • Use the angle-of-incidence framing to reason about whether sound penetrates or skips off a thermal boundary
  • Identify when multiple delay clusters are preferable to shooting one system over a long distance
