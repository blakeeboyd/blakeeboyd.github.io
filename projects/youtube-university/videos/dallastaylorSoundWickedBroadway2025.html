Takeaway

Josh Maszle, head of audio for Wicked on Broadway, gives a tour of the rig and his mixing approach. He covers the hidden Meyer PA built around the goal of making the show sound sourced on stage rather than reinforced, the 36-pack Countryman B6 setup with redundant rigs for principals, the 23-piece orchestra mic'd "like an orchestra" with air between sources, and the 16-mix Aviom personal monitor system for the pit. He works on a Cadac J-Type, one of the last analog consoles on Broadway, using VCAs and MIDI-triggered Kontakt cues for sound effects. He closes by contrasting his first show (sweaty palms, just hit the right faders at the right time) with his current approach (anticipating what each performer needs, second by second).

Questions

  1. 1.Sound designer Tony Meola hides the speakers behind set elements and aims for sound that feels "sourced on stage." What workflow choices does Maszle describe (mic placement, foldback policy, deck speakers) that support this aesthetic, and what would change if the show prioritized maximum clarity instead?Analyze

    Choices supporting "sourced on stage": speakers hidden behind set pieces (no visible source); no vocal foldback (vocals never come through stage monitors, only what naturally reflects back); deck speakers timed precisely to match the actor's position; orchestra mic'd from a distance with air between sources (not close-mic'd and panned). If the show prioritized maximum clarity instead, you'd see close-miked instruments isolated and panned wherever the mix wanted them, with louder stage foldback so actors could rely on monitor information. Trade-off: clarity at the cost of the spatial illusion that the music lives on stage.

  2. 2.Maszle still mixes on a Cadac J-Type analog console while most Broadway shows have moved to digital. What does he gain by staying analog, what does he give up, and how do features like VCAs and MIDI-triggered Kontakt cues compensate for what a digital console would do automatically?Evaluate

    Analog gains: warmer sound (voltage-domain summing without conversion artifacts), no latency, no menu diving. Hands stay on the controls. Analog loses: no recallable EQ per channel, no input compression or gating, every tonal decision lives on a physical knob set by hand. VCAs (per-channel level recall via group fader scenes) let him recall fader positions even though EQ stays static. MIDI-triggered Kontakt cues fire sound effects from console scene changes, so cue-driven effects work like a digital console even though dynamics processing is still hand-tuned. He still mixes the show by hand on the faders, which is the analog texture he's preserving.

  3. 3.The orchestra is mic'd "like an orchestra, not like a band" with each mic capturing a little air and bleed from neighboring instruments. Why is this approach used for Wicked, and what mixing problems does it create compared to close-mic'd, isolated tracks?Analyze

    Mic'ing like an orchestra means each mic captures its primary instrument plus a little of the neighbors and a little of the room, preserving the bleed and spatial cues that make an orchestra sound like an orchestra rather than 23 isolated instruments stacked together. Problems this creates: less independent control (turning up the cello brings up some of the violin and viola); more potential for phase issues if mics are repositioned; you can't replace the violin mic without affecting everything around it. The trade-off is preserving the ensemble blend that close-mic'd isolation would destroy.

  4. 4.Maszle contrasts his first show ("push the right faders, push the right VCAs, push the button at the right time, just get through the show cleanly") with his current mixing ("being totally in touch with what they're doing on stage"). What specific examples does he give of this performer-attuned mixing, and what does this suggest about what experience adds to the job?Analyze

    Specific examples Maszle gives: anticipating a singer's throat-clearing and dipping their mic in advance; noticing the orchestra falling behind and giving the singer more orchestra in the foldback for a moment; sensing when a performer needs a slight push of music underneath a vulnerable line. None of these are in the script or cue sheet. They're real-time reads of the performer's state. Experience adds the ability to mix from anticipation rather than reaction. The new engineer is following a cue list; the experienced one is in the show with the performer.
