Takeaway

Most system-tuning tutorials default to measurement-mic-and-pink-noise as the industry standard. Sheets keeps that method in the toolbox but spends the bulk of the video on a by-ear approach built around a personal default EQ that mirrors the Fletcher-Munson curves. The connection between published psychoacoustic research and a practical mix-bus EQ is what makes the video unusual. He walks through the standard methods (feedback avoidance, comparison to a known reference, flat tuning with pink noise) before turning to his own, frames replication under similar conditions as the actual marker of mastery, and closes with a warning that engineers who never tune without measurement software eventually lose the ability to hear at all.

Questions

  1. 1.Sheets proposes a 2x2 of vision and skill, yielding four kinds of engineers. Place yourself in one of the four cells and explain what would have to change for you to move toward "strong vision, strong skill."Apply

    The four cells are: (1) no vision, no skills (no idea what they're going for, accepts whatever sound comes up); (2) vision, no skills (knows how it should sound but cannot get there); (3) skills, no vision (tweaks for days but produces inconsistent results); (4) both, which produces consistency. A good answer will name a cell, give a specific example from the student's own work, and identify the missing axis. Weak skill is fixed by repetition (Sheets's EQ drill in Q3); weak vision is fixed by critical listening to reference tracks and learning to verbalize what you hear before touching a knob.

  2. 2.What is the connection between the Fletcher-Munson curves and the "default channel EQ" Sheets developed? Why does he think tuning a system this way might work better than tuning flat?Analyze

    Fletcher-Munson curves show that human ears are not flat across frequency. At moderate volumes the midrange (especially around 3-4 kHz) is perceived as louder than the lows and highs at the same SPL. A measurement-flat PA therefore sounds midrange-heavy to a listener. Sheets's default EQ does roughly the inverse of the Fletcher-Munson sensitivity curve (cuts around 400 Hz and 2.5 kHz, boosts the sub-50 Hz region), so what arrives at the audience matches what a flat reference would sound like inside a listener's head rather than at a measurement mic.

  3. 3.Sheets describes a concrete ear-training exercise involving a friend, an EQ plugin, and pink noise. Describe the exercise in your own words and explain what specific skill it builds.Understand

    A friend sets an EQ on a familiar reference signal (pink noise or a known song, in Pro Tools or Logic) without showing the student. The student then adjusts their own EQ to undo the change. They compare afterward; the two EQs should be exact opposites. Done daily for a month, the exercise builds frequency identification (the ability to hear "this is boosted around 2 kHz" without a meter), and eventually trains the student to imagine the corrective EQ shape before touching any knobs.

  4. 4.When does Sheets still reach for flat tuning with a measurement mic, and when does he tune by ear? What is his rationale for the distinction?Analyze

    He uses flat tuning when he is the system tech for a festival where touring engineers will load their USB sticks expecting a measurement-flat PA so their mixes translate predictably. He uses by-ear when he is mixing the show himself (about 95% of his work), because the by-ear default EQ does most of the work a flat system would later need on every channel, freeing channel EQ bands for material specific to that show. The rationale is consistency-of-translation: flat is for guests; by-ear is for personal mixing.
