Do you know how subwoofers really work?

Alpha Sound · 09:20 · Applied

Watch on YouTube ↗

Takeaway

A measurement-based companion to Sheets's later bass-physics video. He sets two Nexo subs in the snow (an RS18 front-loaded dual cabinet and an LS18 dual-ported cabinet) and runs noise and tone through them while a mic captures the driver side and the port side separately. The frequency-response traces show where each side resonates, and the phase traces show why the cabinet sums above tuning and cancels below it: at low frequencies the port moves opposite the driver in a push-pull relationship, but past the resonance point the air inside the cabinet starts behaving like ripples on a pond rather than a stream of water, and the phase relationship inverts. He closes with the asymmetry that matters most for cabinet design: cancellation needs both 180 degree phase AND matched volume, while summation is gradual, so the shape of the final response is governed as much by relative loudness as by phase.

Questions

  1. 1.Sheets calls the face of the driver the "positive side" and the port the "negative side." Below the cabinet's tuning frequency, what is the physical motion at each side, and why does he describe their relationship as 180 degrees?Understand

    The push-pull motion is the key: when the driver moves outward and produces positive pressure in front of itself, an equal volume of air is sucked into the cabinet through the port, so the port radiates negative pressure (rarefaction) at the same instant the driver radiates positive pressure (compression), the exact 180-degree opposition. Below tuning the air inside the cabinet does not yet resonate, so this simple opposition holds and the two outputs cancel.

  2. 2.The RS18 has a single internal chamber per driver, while the LS18 encloses the driver in its own chamber and ports it through a second chamber. How does that structural difference show up in the phase plots, especially the additional resonance the LS18 has around 75 Hz?Analyze

    The RS18 phase plot has one main resonance flip near 32 Hz and a second near 200 Hz, with the driver and port in phase between them, because there is only one resonating chamber per driver. The LS18 has an additional resonance from the driver-side chamber around 75 Hz, which Sheets argues happens because the smaller chamber has a higher resonant frequency than the larger port-side chamber. The 75 Hz phase flip comes from the positive side (the driver chamber), not the port chamber, and that this is what creates the messy alternating summing and cancellation pattern the LS18 shows in the mid-80 to 200 Hz range.

  3. 3.Sheets says that once air inside a cabinet begins to resonate, it stops behaving like a stream of water and starts behaving like ripples on a pond. Walk through what that analogy is doing for the argument, and connect it to the observed phase flip at resonance.Analyze

    The analogy distinguishes two regimes for air motion. Below resonance, air in the port behaves like a stream of water: the molecules themselves move from one place to another, so the port output is directly tied to the driver pulling and pushing it. Above resonance, the air behaves like ripples on a pond: the disturbance propagates but the air molecules stay roughly in place, so the port output is no longer mechanically tied to the driver in opposition, and the phase relationship inverts from 180 degrees to 0 degrees as the cabinet passes through resonance.

  4. 4.At the end Sheets argues that cancellation requires both 180 degree phase AND matched volume between the two sources, while summation is more gradual. Use this asymmetry to justify why processing (high-pass and low-pass filters) is the standard fix for the raw responses he measured, rather than mechanical redesign of the cabinet.Evaluate

    Cancellation has a narrow operating window: phase must be 180 degrees AND the two volumes must be close in level. Summation tolerates a much wider range of both. The implication for cabinet design is that the raw responses Sheets measured have several phase flips that should produce cancellation, but because the port and driver outputs are unequal in level at those frequencies, the cancellations are partial and the curve does not collapse. Credit responses that argue high-pass and low-pass filters work because they push the cabinet's natural deep dips and resonances outside the operational band, leaving only the wide, forgiving summation region in the middle. Mechanical redesign would have to fix every resonance individually; filter-based shaping uses the asymmetry to cover up the worst features cheaply.
