Takeaway

Curtis walks through how to align a main-to-sub crossover using simulated trace data in Open Sound Meter, removing the variables of microphone placement and field conditions. He uses a QSC KW153 main and KS118 sub from tracebook.org as the example pair, defines a target trace (Michael Lawrence), and applies Bob McCarthy's three-zone framework (combing, transition, isolation) to argue that alignment only matters within the 10 dB window where the two speakers are within 10 dB of each other. The alignment criterion is phase-slope matching through that window, not amplitude matching, and Merlin van Bean's phase wheel says ±45° of phase offset still produces +5 dB summation, so you don't need to chase perfect alignment. He uses Open Sound Meter's math source (vector sum) to predict the combined response.

Before you watch

  • Awareness of crossover frequencies and how they work between main and sub speakers
  • Basic understanding of phase as a frequency-domain measurement

Questions

  1. 1.Curtis applies Bob McCarthy's three-zone framework (combing, transition, isolation) to argue that main-to-sub alignment only matters within the "10 dB window" where the two sources are within 10 dB of each other. Explain why he doesn't need to align outside that window, using his Godzilla-vs-Bambi example.Analyze

    The 10 dB window is the band of frequencies where the main and sub are within 10 dB of each other in level. Below that window, the sub dominates and the main is too quiet to interfere meaningfully; above, the main dominates and the sub is too quiet. Bob McCarthy's framing: if two speakers are more than ~10 dB apart, the quieter one is "Bambi" to the louder "Godzilla" and they don't interact audibly.

  2. 2.Curtis says the alignment criterion is the phase slope matching between main and sub through the crossover region, not the amplitude matching. Why is phase slope the right thing to match, and what would happen if you tried to align by amplitude alone?Analyze

    Phase slope is the timing relationship across the crossover region: how the main and sub line up in time at each frequency. Amplitude alignment alone (making both at the same level) just tells you they're within the interaction zone. It doesn't tell you whether they sum constructively or destructively. If their phase is 180° apart, they cancel even at matched amplitude. If their phase slopes match through the window, they sum constructively across all frequencies in the band. Matching by amplitude without matching phase can produce a system that measures "balanced" but cancels at the crossover.

  3. 3.Curtis uses Merlin van Bean's phase wheel: ±45° of phase offset still produces +5 dB of summation (vs. +6 dB for perfectly in-phase). Apply this rule to a situation where your measured phase trace shows a 30° drift across the crossover window. Is that "good enough"? What if it drifts 60°?Apply

    30° drift: within the ±45° tolerance, so summation is at least +5 dB (close to the +6 dB ideal). This is good enough; no further tweaking needed unless you have time. 60° drift: outside the ±45° threshold. At 60° phase offset, summation drops to ~+4 dB and the off-axis behavior gets worse. Worth tweaking the sub delay to pull the slope into the ±45° window.

  4. 4.The vector sum (math source) in OSM lets Curtis predict the combined main+sub response without measuring the actual sum. What does the math source compute, and what real-world variables would still differ between this simulation and the actual measured sum at a given audience seat?Analyze

    The vector sum takes the two transfer functions (main and sub, each with magnitude and phase) and computes their predicted complex sum at each frequency: amplitude + amplitude_with_phase_offset. It assumes the two sources are at the same point in space. Real-world variables that differ at an audience seat: the distance from the listener to the main vs. to the sub introduces additional phase shift (Curtis acknowledges this is the "what about microphone placement" question for part 2); the room contributes reflections that change the apparent magnitude and phase; speakers don't sum identically at every position in the audience.
