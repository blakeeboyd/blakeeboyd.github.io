Phase, Polarity, and Decorrelation

A theoretical foundation in phase, polarity, and decorrelation, applied to live PA practice

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Comfortable with basic EQ controls (frequency, gain, Q)
  • Familiar with stereo PAs in concept
  • Some exposure to live or studio mixing

By the end you'll be able to

  • Distinguish phase, polarity, and time delay from one another
  • Explain why two pink-noise sources don't comb-filter while one source to two speakers does
  • Apply decorrelation principles to dual-mic'd instruments in a live PA
  • Trace the connection from phase theory to live-sound comb filtering and feedback

Course overview

Phase is one of the most consistently misunderstood concepts in audio. Engineers conflate it with time delay, fear it as a side effect of EQ, and miss its central role in why live PA systems comb-filter the way they do. This course walks from the abstract DSP version of phase (Dan Worrall's three videos) through phase as a physical reality in microphone capsules, then applies the same theory to live sound reinforcement (Dave Rat's decorrelation work).

By the end you should be able to look at a phase trace and read it, predict whether two correlated sources will comb-filter at a given audience seat, and decide whether decorrelating your mics will help.

Module 1: Phase fundamentals

The first two videos establish that phase shift is not the same as time delay, and that EQ doesn't cause phase shift as a side effect: phase shift causes EQ.

09:17

Delay Always Means Phase Shift But Phase Shift Does Not Always Mean Delay

Dan Worrall

Applied

Worrall demonstrates three ways to produce 180° of phase shift (polarity inversion, time delay, all-pass filter) and shows that they sound identical at one frequency but behave differently at every other frequency. The clincher: a sharp spike at the end of the test tone passes through the all-pass filter with its peak amplitude almost doubled, which explains why an EQ cut can sometimes make a track louder.

Then

Worrall ended the first video with an open question: how does an all-pass filter manage to shift the phase of certain frequencies while leaving amplitude flat? The next video answers this by showing that EQ filters are built out of all-pass filters and arithmetic.

04:22

EQ Doesn't Cause Phase Shift...

Dan Worrall

Applied

Worrall derives every standard EQ filter shape (low-pass, high-pass, shelf, bell) from an all-pass filter plus addition or subtraction. The punchline reverses the conventional question: EQ doesn't cause phase shift, phase shift causes EQ.

Checkpoint Quiz 1: Phase fundamentals

  1. Q1. Which of the following is true about a 5 ms delay applied to a 100 Hz signal vs. the same 5 ms delay applied to a 200 Hz signal? Multiple choice

  2. Q2. Worrall builds a high-shelf filter using an all-pass filter, subtraction, and addition. Explain in your own words why this arithmetic produces a frequency-selective gain change even though the all-pass filter has a flat magnitude response. Short answer

    The all-pass shifts phase increasingly across the cutoff frequency, ending at 180° in the high end. Subtracting the all-pass from the dry signal: at low frequencies (no phase shift) the subtraction cancels everything, while at high frequencies (180° shift) the subtraction adds, producing a high-pass shape. Scaling that high-pass and adding it to the dry signal reinforces only the high frequencies. The selectivity comes from how the phase shift varies across the spectrum, not from any amplitude change in the all-pass itself.

  3. Q3. Worrall's claim is that EQ doesn't cause phase shift; phase shift causes EQ. What does this reframing change about how an engineer should think about minimum-phase EQ? Synthesis

    The conventional framing treats phase shift as an unfortunate side effect that linear-phase EQ exists to "fix." Worrall's reframing reveals that phase shift is the mechanism by which EQ works at all: the all-pass that produces a shelf or a bell is doing the phase work, and the amplitude change is a downstream consequence. This means minimum-phase EQ phase shifts aren't damage to be repaired but are inherent to the operation. It also explains why "transparent" EQs that promise zero phase shift have to use linear-phase topologies, which introduce pre-ringing as a different trade-off.

Module 2: Phase in the physical world

So far phase has been an abstract DSP concept inside Reaper and Reaktor. The next video shows that the same 90° phase relationships exist in physical microphone capsules.

08:24

Phascinating Phacts about Phase

Dan Worrall

Applied

Worrall reveals that dynamic and condenser microphones are 90° out of phase even when their capsules touch. The reason: a dynamic mic is a velocity transducer (it measures air movement), while a condenser is a pressure transducer (it measures air pressure). Velocity and pressure are 90° apart because pressure peaks when velocity drops to zero. He closes by deriving the same 90° phase shift in software using only a one-sample rate-of-change calculation, tying the physical and the mathematical together.

Checkpoint Quiz 2: Phase in the physical world

  1. Q1. A dynamic mic and a condenser mic are placed at exactly the same distance from a vocalist. What is the phase relationship between their outputs? Multiple choice

  2. Q2. If you wanted to "fix" the 90° offset between a dynamic and condenser by sliding one mic forward or backward, why does this fail? Short answer

    Sliding the mic adds a time delay, which creates a frequency-dependent phase shift on top of the 90° pressure/velocity offset. You could find a distance where the two outputs align at one specific frequency, but at every other frequency the added delay would introduce comb filtering. You'd trade one phase problem for many.

  3. Q3. Worrall's refrain is "delay always means phase shift, but phase shift does not always mean delay." Using the dynamic-vs-condenser example, explain what kind of phase shift this 90° offset is (not delay) and what makes it impossible to correct with timing. Synthesis

    The 90° offset between dynamic and condenser mics is a frequency-independent phase shift that comes from measuring different physical quantities (velocity vs. pressure), not from any path-length difference. Both capsules receive the air wave at the same instant; the offset is in what they convert from the wave. Time delay is the wrong tool because timing-based phase shift is frequency-dependent (different frequencies get different shifts from the same delay). You cannot use a frequency-dependent tool to fix a frequency-independent offset without introducing new frequency-dependent problems.

Module 3: Decorrelation in practice

Now we leave the studio and head to live PA, where phase summation between correlated sources becomes the dominant tonality issue an engineer has to manage.

12:30

Decorrelation Demo - Reddit Respond Episode 5

Dave Rat

Applied

Rat puts two mics (Royer ribbon and Audix OM7) on the same flat-panel speaker, EQ's them to match, and demonstrates that polarity-reversing one mic produces less cancellation than polarity-reversing the same mic sent to both speakers. The two mics are partially correlated: they share the source but differ in pickup pattern and EQ. Partial correlation means the comb-filter problems don't stack at the same frequencies for every listener.

Then

Decorrelation is more than a parlor trick: it's a principle that explains why so much of live sound reinforcement is fighting unnatural phenomena. The next video pulls the principle up to its general framing.

08:07

Sound Systems are Completely Unnatural

Dave Rat

Foundational

Rat names two ways sound reinforcement is unnatural: the same sound radiating from multiple speakers (stereo PA) creates comb filtering, and multiple sounds from one speaker compresses spatial separation. Both are fixable by exploiting correlation: line arrays use controlled correlation to steer dispersion; cardioid sub arrays use controlled cancellation to direct bass. The dual-mic technique from video 4 is the practical version of the same principle applied to source decorrelation.

Checkpoint Quiz 3: Decorrelation in practice

  1. Q1. Two pink-noise sources played from two speakers produce no comb filtering, even with one polarity-reversed. Why? Multiple choice

  2. Q2. Rat hard-pans a Royer ribbon (front of a guitar cab) and an Audix OM7 (back of the same cab, polarity-reversed) across the stereo PA. Why does this produce smoother coverage across the audience than running a single mic to both sides? Short answer

    A single mic sent to both PA sides is perfectly correlated, so every audience seat hears the same comb-filter pattern at the same frequencies as they move across the venue. Hard-panning two different mics on the same source decorrelates the signal: the comb filtering still exists for each mic, but the cancellation frequencies are different for the two mic signals because their pickup patterns and EQ differ. From any audience seat, the cancellations from mic A occur at slightly different frequencies than the cancellations from mic B. The result is broader-band thinning rather than complete cancellation at specific frequencies.

  3. Q3. Rat says line arrays and cardioid sub arrays "weaponize" the same correlation effect that causes comb filtering when handled carelessly. Pick one (line array or cardioid sub) and explain how the engineer is using correlation constructively rather than fighting it. Synthesis

    A line array stacks identical drivers vertically. The identical signal from each driver is perfectly correlated, which is exactly what causes comb filtering in the wrong context. In a line array, the engineer uses that correlation deliberately: horizontally the drivers couple into a coherent single source (smooth coverage side-to-side), and vertically the same correlation produces destructive interference at angles above and below the array's aim (sound cuts off above the audience and below their feet). The engineer chooses the geometry so the unavoidable comb-filter cancellations happen where there's no audience to hear them. OR A cardioid sub array uses two correlated subs at quarter-wavelength spacing with one digitally delayed by a quarter cycle. The forward sub and the delayed sub arrive in phase forward of the array (constructive summation) but out of phase behind it (cancellation). Same correlation physics; the timing offset reshapes where the cancellations happen.
